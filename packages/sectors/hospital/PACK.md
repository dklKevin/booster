# hospital sector package

Use this pack for hospital, health-system, specialty-care, and care-access websites where clinical credibility and a clear patient action must coexist.
Load with the form package matching the page's job, plus 2-3 refs total chosen for the brief.
Refs teach how to think, never what to build.

## The hospital mode (avoid)

- Avoid the full-bleed staff, bedside-family, or clinician-and-patient hero with a dark gradient or translucent copy panel, an aspirational care promise, and an appointment, doctor, location, or treatment CTA.
- Avoid defaulting to institutional navy and clinical blue - such as `#03173E` with `#0077B5`, `#213245` with `#095f7e`, or `#0057b8` with `#012d61` - on white or pale gray, then assigning one orange, cyan, chartreuse, or magenta accent to actions.
- Avoid the long modular homepage stack of care-entry cards, alternating image-and-copy features, news grids or carousels, location cards, research or education pillars, and a final statistics band.
- Avoid making “Find a doctor,” “Appointments,” and “Locations” the same three-card or three-button gateway immediately below the hero.
- Avoid treating polished people-centered care photography, campus thumbnails, lab scenes, medical equipment, and flat service icons as a complete visual point of view.
- Avoid staging trust as a single ranking claim, executive or researcher testimonial, generic mission language, and system-scale counts for employees, physicians, locations, or institutes.
- Avoid the oversized navy or charcoal institutional footer that repeats audience directories, departments, social channels, phone numbers, patient rights, price transparency, and legal or compliance links.
- Never place the red cross emblem (or red crescent/crystal) on staff, signage, or iconography: it is legally protected under the Geneva Conventions, real healthcare organizations are forced to remove it, and it reads as medical clipart. Use the practice's own mark or a neutral badge. (Observed failure: E10-B2 dressed all five illustrated clinicians in red crosses, 2026-08-08.)

## Tells

- A warm caregiver or family photograph carries an institutional reassurance line over a dark gradient.
- Blue-and-white cards immediately split the journey into provider, appointment, location, portal, or urgent-care funnels.
- A national ranking or system-scale statistic is presented as the primary proof of quality.
- A dense department-and-compliance footer outweighs the content directly above it.

## Register

- Credibility is load-bearing when it helps a person decide or act: accepted insurance, board-certified clinicians, care outcomes, top-rated doctors, wait time, appointment availability, advanced testing, referral routes, and clearly named locations all do more work than a generic badge strip.
- Rankings, system size, mission statements, executive signatures, and campus imagery are supporting context, not substitutes for service-specific evidence or a usable care path.
- The audience expects calm, plain, reassuring language with precise operational labels for finding care, booking, referrals, treatments, insurance, and urgent needs.
- Excellence proves that clinical authority does not require hospital blue; a derived palette carries it when text contrast is strong and action colors keep strict semantic roles.
- Excellence also proves that trust can feel editorial and human, with proof distributed through the narrative instead of isolated in one institutional panel.
- Distinct audiences should retain distinct first-viewport routes - such as member care versus platform exploration, or referral versus research versus donation - without collapsing every intent into a mega-menu.
- Photography is load-bearing in this sector: real clinics show their people, spaces, and equipment, and a photo-less page reads as a brand exercise, not a place where care happens. Escape the cliched caregiver-gradient composition, never photography itself.
- This audience reads in light; a dark theme is not part of the patient-facing register.
- A real practice site is mostly operational surface, and patients recognize its absence instantly: patient portal or records access, new-patient forms and what to bring, patient education for the conditions treated, insurance and billing detail including self-pay, languages and accessibility, privacy notices, and plain after-hours and emergency guidance. A site with only the photogenic pages reads as a brochure mockup, not a practice.
- Density is part of clinical credibility: real clinic pages carry more information per viewport than editorial pages, and patients trust the site that answers the third and fourth question, not just the first.
- **Operational surface:** keep portal or records access, new-patient intake, insurance and self-pay, condition education, after-hours guidance, and named locations on the owned site, as Zocdoc leads with specialty, location, and insurance search and One Medical keeps care taxonomy actionable.
- **Operational consistency:** one current source for hours, locations, accepted insurance, and booking destinations; a brochure homepage that hands off to a contradictory portal fails the visit.
- **Photography:** show the people, rooms, and equipment that belong to this practice. Escape the caregiver-gradient hero, never photography itself.
